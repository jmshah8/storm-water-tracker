Method

How each discharge is recorded and classified, what the method can and cannot tell you, and every source it relies on.

The definition

“{{ ea_rule }}”

Environment Agency blog, “What are dry day spills?”, 28 August 2024. The same post says “Storm overflows should not spill on dry days, but there are exceptions.” and that the Environment Agency uses “rainfall data from local rain gauges as well as rainfall radar information”; when it finds dry day spills it will “treat them as a potential breach until we have confirmed through further investigation”. Every classification on this site implements that one sentence, and nothing else.

How we apply it

  • What is classified. The start of each discharge. The day that matters is the UTC calendar day on which the discharge started. A discharge that started in rain and carried on into a dry day is not flagged.
  • The window. “That day and the preceding 24 hours” is read as the 48 hours from 00:00 UTC on the day before the start to 00:00 UTC on the day after it.
  • The rain. We add up every 15-minute rainfall total recorded in that window at the chosen rain gauge.
  • The verdict. The discharge is a dry day spill if, and only if, the window total is 0.25 mm or less. Every flag carries the words “Potential breach; not confirmed.”, because only the Environment Agency can confirm a breach.
  • Other outcomes. “Not a dry day” when the total is above 0.25 mm; “Rain check pending” while rainfall may still arrive; “Insufficient rain data” when no gauge within 10 km returned enough readings in time; “No gauge within 10 km” when there is none, which cannot be classified under this method.
  • Length. Each flagged discharge shows how long it lasted, worked out from the start and end times the company published (both to the second). If a company has not published a valid end time, the page says so instead.

Why “total of 0.25 mm or less”

The Environment Agency's sentence can be read two ways: no single reading above 0.25 mm, or no more than 0.25 mm of rain in all. We use the total. It is the stricter reading for a flag: a window with, say, three readings of 0.2 mm has no single reading above 0.25 mm but a total of 0.6 mm, so it is flagged under the other reading and not under ours. Using the total therefore flags fewer discharges and minimises false flags. Both numbers, the window total and the largest single 15-minute reading, are published for every event, so the other reading can be recomputed from our data.

Which rain gauge

We use the Environment Agency's rain gauges from its Hydrology API, which publishes 15-minute rainfall totals. For each overflow we consider every gauge within 10 km (straight-line distance from the coordinates the company publishes) and use the nearest one that returned at least 176 of the 192 readings expected over the 48 hours (91.7%). The 10 km limit is our own assumption, chosen so that the gauge is reasonably representative of rain at the overflow; the Environment Agency does not publish a distance. Overflows with no gauge within 10 km are shown as “No gauge within 10 km” and are not classified. {{ n_no_coords }} overflows in the feeds currently have no coordinates; they cannot be matched to a gauge and are counted the same way.

Gauges that have stopped reporting

A rain gauge that fails usually fails silently: a blocked funnel or a dead sensor reports 0.00 mm however hard it rains, which looks exactly like dry weather and would produce a flag carrying our strongest evidence line. We therefore skip a gauge that has stopped reporting rain, in the same way we skip one with too few readings.

A gauge counts as stopped, for a given day, when over the previous 30 days it has at least 5 days of near-complete readings and recorded 0.00 mm on every one of them, and something independent says it rained in that time: either at least two gauges within 20 km recorded more than 1 mm, or the rainfall radar recorded more than 1 mm over the overflow itself. Two independent gauges are required so that one faulty neighbour, or one very local shower, is not enough. When this happens we move to the next-nearest gauge, and the event's page says how many gauges were skipped. Radar is used here only to corroborate that a gauge has died; it never adjusts a working gauge's readings. This is the single place where radar can touch a verdict at all, by changing which gauge is used — so we test it: re-classifying every event twice, once with the radar files present and once with them removed, produces identical verdicts for all {{ n_events|thin }} of them.

Time zone

We use UTC calendar days. The Environment Agency's definition does not specify a time zone; EDM data is reported in GMT. The company feeds give times as UTC. The Hydrology API gives rainfall times without a time zone, so we checked: on 15 September 2026, a British Summer Time day, eight gauges with 9.3 to 38.2 mm of rain matched the Environment Agency's real-time rainfall API (whose times are marked UTC) on 96 of 96 fifteen-minute readings with no offset, and disagreed by 10 to 35 mm when shifted by one hour. Hydrology API times are therefore UTC.

Provisional and final verdicts

A verdict is final when it can no longer change: when there is no gauge within 10 km; when a dry day or not-dry verdict is based on all 192 of 192 readings and at least 72 hours have passed since the window ended; or, with any number of readings, once 14 days have passed since the window ended. Until then a verdict is provisional and is re-checked every hour, because the Environment Agency sends some readings late. A flag shows how many of the 192 readings it is based on: “Complete rain data (192 of 192 readings).” when none are missing; “awaiting” and the number of readings still missing while they may yet arrive, because a late reading that recorded rain could change the verdict; and “final” with the number never published once 14 days have passed. Every change between “dry day spill” and “not a dry day” is recorded in a public log (verdict_changes.csv).

How long it takes

Companies must publish a discharge within an hour of it starting, and we read the feeds every ten minutes, so a discharge usually appears here within about an hour. The rainfall arrives later: the Hydrology API was about half a day behind when this site began, and a verdict also has to wait until the 48-hour window has ended. A verdict therefore typically appears one to three days after the discharge started. While rain may still arrive (up to 72 hours after the window ends) the event shows “Rain check pending”. The site itself is republished on a schedule (at least weekly), so its figures can lag the data files by up to that long; the footer shows when each part of the data last changed.

Radar as a second opinion

Rain gauges measure the rain that falls into one funnel, up to 10 km from the overflow. Rainfall radar estimates rain over every square kilometre, including the overflow's own, by measuring how much of its beam the raindrops bounce back. The two are independent, and the Environment Agency uses both: it says it checks “rainfall data from local rain gauges as well as rainfall radar information”.

For each flagged discharge we add up Met Office radar rainfall over the same 48-hour window, for the overflow's own square kilometre and for the nine squares around it, and show it beside the gauge evidence. If the highest of those nine squares is 0.25 mm or less, the radar agrees with the flag; if it is more, the radar disagrees. A disagreement means look closer, not that the flag is wrong: the verdict follows the Environment Agency's definition, which is written around rainfall measurements, and the radar is an estimate that can be thrown off by hills, distance from the radar or heavy showers nearby. Both numbers are published so you can judge for yourself. Radar data contains Met Office radar data © British Crown copyright, CC BY-SA.

What this method can miss

  • The feeds show only each overflow's most recent discharge. If a discharge starts and ends between two of our ten-minute reads and another discharge starts before the next read, the first one is never seen.
  • If we never see a discharge's end, because the next discharge had already started when we looked, its end is taken as the start of the next one and marked as inferred.
  • Where a company publishes an end time earlier than the start time, we leave the end blank rather than guess. Where a monitor comes back online without a published time, the end of its offline period is the time we saw it back, and is labelled as such in the data.
  • A single gauge up to 10 km away can miss a local shower, or record one that missed the overflow. The rainfall radar comparison planned for this site is meant to catch such cases.
  • Monitors can be offline; an overflow that discharges while its monitor is offline does not appear in the feed.

Context

The legal duty to publish comes from the Environment Act 2021: “The information referred to in subsection (1)(a) to (c) must be published within an hour of the discharge beginning; and that referred to in subsection (1)(d) within an hour of it ending.” The Environment Agency's annual Event Duration Monitoring returns count spills per overflow but have no per-event times, and the House of Commons Library notes that “there is at present no way to distinguish 'dry spills' (discharge of sewage at times of little to no rainfall, normally in breach of permit conditions) within this dataset.” The Environment Agency has said that “since January, all day dry spills – no matter how small – are now classified as pollution incidents”, and its permit guidance classes storm overflows as unsatisfactory when they “operate in dry weather conditions”. For 2025 it reported: “There were 291,492 spill events in 2025, a 35% reduction on 2024.” and “Every single storm overflow in England now has an event duration monitor fitted, providing the most complete national picture to date.” The Guardian reported: “In 2025, water companies in England and Wales reported 8,576 dry spill pollution discharges.”

Other projects

Other sites also publish storm overflow data. River Truth (Nexfort Data Limited) flags “dry-weather spills” per event using its own rule (“Spill occurred with effectively no rainfall in the preceding 24 h”), without a published rainfall threshold or source. Top of the Poops publishes annual monitoring data alongside the live feeds, with rainfall shown in aggregate. The Rivers Trust and Surfers Against Sewage publish maps and alerts of spills. This site differs in applying the Environment Agency's own definition exactly, keeping a forward record of every discharge, and publishing per-company totals, the evidence for each flag and the full data.

Rule changelog

  • Counting change, not a rule change (20 Sep 2026): flags the radar materially contradicts are counted in their own column rather than in the headline totals, as described above. No verdict changed and the rule version did not move, because the rule did not change — only what the site adds up.
  • dry-day-v3 (20 Sep 2026): where no rain gauge lies within 10 km of an overflow, the three closest gauges within 20 km are now triangulated instead of the discharge being left without a verdict. This follows the only published regulatory instruction on the point, Natural Resources Wales guidance note GN066: “Use rain gauge data that is the most representative for the SO. Where there is no nearby rain gauge, the three closest gauges can be triangulated.” The three window totals are combined by inverse distance, so the nearest of them counts for most. An overflow that does have a gauge within 10 km still uses that gauge alone. The window, the 0.25 mm threshold and the completeness bar are unchanged.
  • dry-day-v2 (18 Sep 2026): gauges that have stopped reporting rain are skipped when choosing which gauge to use, as described above. The window, the 0.25 mm threshold and everything else are unchanged. Every event was re-classified under this version; 12 flags that had rested on such gauges were withdrawn.
  • dry-day-v1 ({{ launch_date }}): first version: nearest gauge within 10 km with at least 176 of 192 readings, flag when the 48-hour window total is 0.25 mm or less. Any change to the window, threshold or gauge selection gets a new version, a new entry here, and a full re-classification.

Flags the radar contests

A dry day flag says the nearest usable rain gauge recorded 0.25 mm or less over 48 hours. Sometimes the Met Office radar, looking at the same two days over the same overflow, saw a good deal more. When it did, we call the flag contested and count it separately from the headline figure, instead of adding it in as though nothing were wrong.

“A good deal more” means the wettest of the nine radar squares around the overflow exceeded 1 mm — four times the rule's own threshold. That bar is deliberate. Across the whole archive the median disagreement is 0.79 mm: most of the time the radar estimate simply lands on the other side of the same line, which is noise, not evidence. Demoting a flag for that would mislead in the opposite direction.

Three things follow, and they matter. A contested flag is still published, still has its own page with the full evidence, and is still in every CSV — nothing is hidden, only counted in its own column. A flag the radar cannot check is not contested: the Met Office archive only reaches back two years, and a discharge from 2023 must not be demoted for something the archive cannot tell us. And a contested flag is not a withdrawn one: the Environment Agency's definition is written around rainfall measurements, and this site implements that definition and nothing else. A contest is a reason to look closer, which is why the number is on the page rather than quietly removed.

One company, four and a half years

Thames Water publishes its own discharge history back to April 2022, which no other company does and the Hub does not hold. Running that history through this same rule is a way of testing the method over years rather than weeks: the Thames Water back-test. It is a test of the method, not an audit of one company, and the page says plainly where it should not be trusted.
